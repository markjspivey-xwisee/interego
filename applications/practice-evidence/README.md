# Practice Evidence: conversation practice with evidence a reviewer can stand behind

> **Vertical application of Interego — NOT part of the protocol, NOT a reference implementation of the protocol.** One specific use case among many possible. The Interego protocol does not depend on any of this; multiple alternative verticals could exist over the same substrate. Vocabulary in this document (`pev:` namespace) is vertical-scoped, non-normative, and lives in [`ontology/pev.ttl`](ontology/pev.ttl).
>
> Status: Working Draft. Layer: application-over-L3. No L1/L2/L3 ontologies are extended.

## What it does

Five kinds of people use it, and an agent can sit in any seat its registry entry allows.

- **Author.** Writes a practice scenario: what the learner practises, their part, the situation, the person they practise with (who they are, what they want, what they know, how they react), that person's first line, when the conversation ends, a turn limit, and a rubric of criteria with ordered levels and a descriptor per level. The author must say how the scenario is used (feedback only, or a pass gate) and how knowable the work is. They can draft with this bridge's model or their own agent; the record says who drafted it. They save a draft, try it themselves (a test that never counts), and publish. Every content edit is a new version that supersedes the old one. Per scenario they see results and, as counts suppressed below five, how often reviewers raised or lowered the judge's level; reviewers and admins also see each change and its reason.
- **Reviewer.** Ratifies a published rubric version (until then, results on it cannot leave as evidence). Works a ranked queue. States a level for every criterion, confirming the judge's or setting another with a reason. On audit-sample attempts, the web UI asks them to choose levels before it reveals the judge's; the review records what the reviewer declares (`sawJudgeLevelsFirst`), and the API does not enforce the order. Re-reviews a contested review.
- **Learner.** Picks a scenario, says whether they are coached, and talks with the other person (see "Who can take part today" for what a learner needs). Every reply shows which model produced it. They end the conversation (the other person may propose the end, never impose it), ask for judging and second opinions, and see per criterion: the level, their own words highlighted where a quote checked out, what did not check out and why, the judge's words labelled as not part of the score, and what the next level asks for, quoted from the rubric. They can contest a review, download their signed record, copy it to their own pod, and remove the transcript from the operator's pod.
- **Judge.** This bridge's model, or an independent judge agent working from a packet. It picks a level, quotes and a rationale per criterion. It never computes a score and never has the last word.
- **Admin.** Registers roles. Can withdraw a review for cause; the withdrawal is recorded and the learner sees why.

Everything is a signed record on a pod. With no model configured the bridge says so (503) and points at the packet route; it never fakes a model.

## How this differs

These are deliberate design choices of this vertical, stated as our own concepts.

- **The model never does arithmetic and never gets the last word.** A judge returns a level, quotes and a rationale per criterion. The platform checks every quote, character for character after a published normalisation, against the learner's own lines, on whole words only (a quote that starts or ends inside a word, such as "acceptable" inside "unacceptable", does not count) and with at least two words; computes any score from the rubric's weights and pass rule, which the judge never sees; and records a Hypothetical attempt bound to the rubric version hash, the transcript hash and the judge disclosure. A quote that does not check out leaves its criterion unverified, and an unverified criterion counts at its lowest level until a reviewer sets it. A criterion put at its lowest level with no quote is marked "no quote needed", not "verified": nothing was checked, and the queue raises a judgment made mostly of those.
- **Nothing leaves as evidence until a qualified person reviews it, on a rubric a person ratified.** A reviewer who is not the learner, not a judge of the attempt, not the reviewer who asked for the judgment being confirmed (or had it judged by a model they chose with their own key), and not the platform states every level in a signed act. That review is an Asserted record that supersedes the Hypothetical one. Unverified criteria must be set by the reviewer. Statements are built only for a reviewed head on a ratified rubric; a projection declined for eligibility is a 409 and leaves an audit row (an untrusted or forked head is a 409 with no row).
- **Classify first, and feedback only is a real choice.** The author declares how knowable the work is. A pass gate on work not read as evident or knowable needs a recorded reason. A feedback-only scenario computes, stores and projects no score at all.
- **The judge is calibrated per criterion, and the queue says where to look.** Every review is an outcome for the judge, per (judge × prompt × rubric version × criterion), measured as agreement with the reviewer (not accuracy) with a Brier score and a mean level distance. The queue ranks attempts by unverified quotes, criteria put at the lowest level with nothing quoted, disagreement between judgments, closeness to the pass mark, the judge's own uncertainty, thin calibration, learner control and the audit sample, and shows the factors with the priority.
- **Several judgments, with agreement shown.** An attempt can be judged up to five times; a repeat by the same judge and prompt measures test-retest consistency.
- **Practice evidence is not capability evidence.** Records and statements say "practice"; the statement verb is `completed`; no credential is issued from practice; the attempt number and whether the learner was coached are recorded. Both are weaker than they look: the attempt number counts attempts by one principal on one scenario (a signing key, or an Interego account; a new key starts again at 1), and coaching is what the learner declared. Each statement says so in its list of what it loses.
- **Who controlled the other person and the judge is recorded.** When the learner's own agent wrote the other person's lines, or the learner chose the judge's model, the record says so and the queue raises the attempt.
- **The learner holds their record.** They can download every version's exact signed bytes, verifiable offline with the substrate's functions alone; copy every version whose graph is still on the operator pod to a pod they name that accepts this bridge's unauthenticated writes; contest a review; and remove the transcript from the operator's pod while every hash and level stays.
- **Your own agent can do the inference.** Routes: this bridge's model (operator key), your own key for one request (used once, never stored), client-required packets that let your agent play the other person or an independent judge agent judge, and a scripted fixture for the offline demo and tests. Every turn and judgment discloses provider, model, key source and route. On the bridge-model and per-request-key routes the model is the one the provider returned, with usage when the provider measured it; on the packet routes the provider and model are what the signer declared (`declaredBy`), with no usage.
- **Authoring is evidence-first.** A rubric version is identified by its hash; editing creates a new version that supersedes the old one; levels are fixed per version and every attempt binds the version it practised.

## Signing as a learner (or in any role)

Every act that changes or reveals a person's record is signed by that person; the signer is the actor and nothing else names them. The web UI offers four ways:

- **Wallet.** A wallet that supports `personal_sign`. One prompt per signed act (session keys are not built; see Deviations). Read views reuse one signed read for up to nine minutes.
- **Paste.** The page shows the exact text to sign (`canonicalJson` of the payload) and takes the pasted signature.
- **Demo.** With `PEV_DEMO_ROLES=1` on a loopback bridge, or the demo's `--serve`, the bridge holds role keys and signs as the role picked. A permanent banner says so, and every record those keys sign says so.
- **Interego account.** With Interego sign-in on, a person with no key signs in with their Interego account (their passkey, on the relay's own page) and the relay signs every act for them, through this bridge. Relay-attested, not user-held: see "Signing in from this bridge's web UI".

Agents use either envelope form: `{payload, signature}` (EIP-191 over `canonicalJson(payload)`), or `{_signed_payload, _signature}` when `agent_id` names the signer's own key. A person with no key of their own signs in with their Interego account instead (next section): then the relay signs, and the bridge reads them as relay-attested, not user-held.

## Sign in with your Interego account (relay-attested, not user-held)

A person who signs in with their Interego account holds no key. From Claude or ChatGPT they call the relay's `act` with `sign_payload: true` (this bridge's web UI has the relay sign the same way: see "Signing in from this bridge's web UI"). The relay signs the payload with its delegation key for the agent of their authenticated session and posts `{_signed_payload, _signature}` to the affordance's target. That signed payload is the caller's fields plus `agent_id` (the session's agent, which the caller cannot choose), `subject_pod_url` (the caller's own pod) and `timestamp` (set by the relay); a caller's `podUrl` arrives as `pod_url`. This bridge then:

1. reads the request as relay-delegated only when its signature (EIP-191 over `sha256:` and the hex sha256 of `_signed_payload`) recovers to an address in `PEV_TRUSTED_RELAY_ADDRESSES`. The same shape signed by any other key is read as a key-held envelope, exactly as before, and refused unless its `agent_id` names that key;
2. checks it as it checks a key-held envelope: `v: 1`, the action it was signed for, freshness (from the relay's `timestamp`, never a caller's `issuedAt`), the nonce's format, the pod (from `pod_url`, never `subject_pod_url`), and, for a write, that the nonce is new;
3. reads the agent's DID document from `https://<PEV_IDENTITY_HOST>/agents/<slug>/did.json` (SSRF-guarded, 8 s in all, the guard's DNS look-ups included, at most 64 KiB, kept 10 minutes) and requires its `id` to be the agent and its `controller` to be a user on the same host, `did:web:<host>:users:<id>`. That user is the **principal**: the person. When the agent's id ends in a canonical user id (`<surface>-u-pk-…`, `-u-eth-…`, `-u-did-…`, as the relay mints them), its controller must be that user: the identity host lets any account register an unused agent id, so one it has forgotten after a restart could be claimed by someone else. The id alone never names a person; the agent's own document is always required.

The principal is then the identity wherever a recovered address is: admins (`PEV_ADMIN_DIDS`, beside `PEV_ADMIN_ADDRESSES`), role registry membership (a member is an address or a person's Interego DID), separation of duties, the replay ledger and the hourly budgets (per person, never per relay address: the relay signs for everyone, and one person through two agents is one learner), attempt keys and the learner, and the actor stored on every record. A DID is compared exactly and never lower-cased, and it never matches an address.

**What this trusts.** The relay key can sign for any agent, and the identity host says whose agent it is. So whoever holds the relay's delegation key, or controls the identity host, can act as any person on this bridge. Every envelope accepted this way is stored with authority `relay-delegated`, the `principal`, the `agentId` and the `relaySigner`, and so is every act on an attempt, so a later reader can tell a relay-attested act from one a person signed with a key they hold. A reader of the pod cannot verify more than that: the relay signature proves the relay signed for that agent; what the identity host said about the agent's controller is in the record only as this platform signed it.

**Re-verification uses no network.** Where the bridge checks a stored envelope again (a review before a projection), a relay-delegated one passes when its signature recovers to its stored `relaySigner`, that address is still in `PEV_TRUSTED_RELAY_ADDRESSES`, the signed `agent_id` is the stored `agentId` on `PEV_IDENTITY_HOST`, and the stored `principal` is a user there. The identity host is not asked again: a bridge that stops listing the relay cannot verify the reviews it attested (the projection is refused, 409 `countersign-not-verifiable`), as a bridge that stops trusting a platform key cannot read its records. `export_record` gives the relay addresses and identity host with the platform addresses, and its verification steps say how to check such an envelope offline. A reader who wants to ask the identity host again fetches the agent's document; its controller may have changed since.

**Decisive acts.** The acts that change who may do what, put a reviewer's name behind a rubric or an attempt, or withdraw a review (`pev.register_role`, `pev.ratify_scenario`, `pev.countersign_attempt`, `pev.override_attempt`, `pev.retract_review`) accept a relay-delegated request only from this bridge's own web UI, an agent whose slug starts with `practice-evidence-` (the relay names an OAuth client's agent `<client name>-<userId>`); a chat agent (`claude-…`, `chatgpt-…`) is refused with 403 `chat-agent-may-not-decide`, naming `PEV_CHAT_AGENTS_DECIDE`, before anything is read from the pod. A ratification makes results on the rubric eligible to leave as evidence, and a countersign takes the judge's levels as the reviewer's own, as an override sets them, so all three are a reviewer's verdict. So is `pev.redact_attempt` when the signer is not the attempt's learner: an admin removing another learner's transcript, which cannot be undone. That is refused the same way once the attempt is read, before anything is written or deleted; a learner removing her own transcript may do so from any agent. `PEV_CHAT_AGENTS_DECIDE=1` lifts the limit. The slug comes from the OAuth client's own name, which any client registering with the relay can choose: the limit keeps a chat agent a person connected for other work from deciding here unasked, and does not prove that this bridge's page made the request. The other acts (authoring, practising, judging, contesting a review, a learner's own copy, export or redaction, and every read) are open to any relay-delegated principal the bridge's rules already allow; they decide nothing for anyone else.

**From Claude.** With the Interego connector, the tool is `act`; `descriptor_url` is this bridge's affordance manifest. The nonce is any 16 to 128 letters, digits, `-` or `_`, new for every write. `pev.list_scenarios` reports the route in `bridge.interegoSignIn` (the relay, the identity host, the trusted relay addresses, the web UI's agent prefix, whether chat agents decide, the decisive acts in `decisiveActs` and `decisiveWhenNotTheLearner`, and what it trusts), or `null` when it is off.

### Signing in from this bridge's web UI

With `PEV_RELAY_URL` set and a relay trusted (the image does both), the web UI offers **Sign in with your Interego account** beside the wallet and paste choices. A person with no wallet:

1. **Presses it.** The page makes a PKCE verifier and a random state, keeps them in this tab's `sessionStorage`, and sends the person to the relay's own sign-in page (the `authorization_endpoint` the bridge reads from `<relay>/.well-known/oauth-authorization-server`, which must be on the relay's origin) with the S256 challenge and the state. There they sign in with their passkey; this bridge never sees it. The page names the relay's host before it goes there.
2. **Comes back** to `<BRIDGE_DEPLOYMENT_URL>/ui/oauth/callback` with a code. The page takes the code out of the address bar and its history first. It refuses an answer whose state is not the one this tab sent, or that reaches a tab that started no sign-in in the last 15 minutes, and keeps nothing from it; a sign-in this tab started goes on waiting for its own answer. An `error` the relay sends back is shown only on the answer to this tab's own sign-in, which it then ends: anyone can send a person to the callback with text of their choosing. Otherwise it posts the code and its verifier to `/ui/oauth/exchange`. The bridge exchanges them at the relay's token endpoint and gives the page the relay's OAuth access token and how long it lasts (`expiresIn`: the relay's `expires_in`, an hour as `deploy/mcp-relay` is written, never more than an hour here and never more than the relay said; one that is missing or not a number is taken as the hour, and one under a second, a token already spent, is refused). It asks the relay for nothing else, and a refresh token the relay sends anyway is dropped. The page keeps the token and its expiry in `sessionStorage` (gone when the tab closes; never `localStorage`) and forgets it at that expiry: a timer drops it while the page is open, and a page loaded later drops it on load and says the sign-in has ended.
3. **Acts.** For each act the page builds the unsigned payload as it does for a wallet and posts it, with the access token as `Authorization: Bearer`, to `/ui/relay/sign`. The bridge posts it to `<relay>/mcp` as one MCP `tools/call` of `sign_request`, with `{payload}` its only argument, a fresh JSON-RPC id and no `initialize` (the relay serves a request with no session as a stateless one), and `Accept: application/json, text/event-stream`. It reads the answer as JSON or as Server-Sent Events and answers only `{_signature, _signed_payload}`, and the page sends those two fields as the act's body. The bridge then reads the act as relay-delegated (above). On `/mcp` the relay names the session's agent itself, from the token: `practice-evidence-<userId>`, since the relay names an OAuth client's agent after its client name, and this client's is "Practice Evidence", so the decisive acts are open to a person signed in here, as the rules allow. A JSON-RPC error, an `isError` result or an `{error}` from the tool is 502 `relay-refused-signing`; a 401 or 403 from the relay is 401 `interego-sign-in-expired`, and the page then forgets the token.
4. **Sees who they are.** The header says **Signed in as `<principal>` via `<agent>`**: the agent from the `agent_id` the relay signed, the principal from the bridge's own answer to a signed read (`pev.get_attempt` answers `signedAs`). The principal DID stands where a wallet's address does, to send to an admin to be given a role. **Sign out** removes the token from the tab, and the next act asks for a pasted signature or a wallet. It revokes nothing, and the page says so: the relay offers no revocation, so the token stays valid at the relay until it expires, and whoever has a copy can use it until then.

**The client.** Its id is `<BRIDGE_DEPLOYMENT_URL>/ui/oauth/client.json`, a Client ID Metadata Document that the relay reads from that URL: `client_name` "Practice Evidence", `redirect_uris` the callback, `grant_types` `authorization_code`, `response_types` `code`, `token_endpoint_auth_method` `none`, scope `mcp`. When the deployment URL is not https (a local run), or the relay does not advertise `client_id_metadata_document_supported`, the bridge registers the same metadata by dynamic client registration instead, once per process, and `/ui/oauth/start` says which (`registration`). The authorization and token requests name the relay as `resource` (RFC 8707). The page asks for no refresh token: rather than keep a 14-day credential in the browser, it uses the access token for as long as the relay makes it last (an hour), and then asks the person to sign in again.

**What the relay sees.** The person signing in, this client, and every payload the page asks it to sign, in plaintext: the act, the pod, the nonce and every field, such as a learner's line in `pev.say` or a reviewer's levels and reasons. `PEV_RECORD_KEY` encrypts records on the pod; it does not hide payloads from the relay. The relay signs each payload for the session's agent and does not see the bridge's answer.

**The relay's access token passes through this bridge.** The page cannot reach the relay itself (its policy is `connect-src 'self'`), so the bridge carries the token to the relay's `/mcp` on every act it signs. It is the relay's OAuth access token for this sign-in (scope `mcp`), and it lasts an hour: until then, whoever holds it can call the relay's tools as that person (publish to their pod, read what they may read, sign as their agent). It is not the identity server's account token, which never reaches this bridge or the page: the bridge calls neither `/identity-token` nor `/tool/<name>`. So what passes through the bridge is the relay session for an hour, not the account. That narrows what the operator is handed, not what an operator who meant harm could reach: the relay's `GET /identity-token` hands the account token (24 hours as `deploy/identity` is written, and good there for linking a wallet, removing sign-in methods and deleting the account) to any holder of an access token with write scope, which signing needs, so an operator who chose to could still trade this token for it during that hour. **So the bridge operator is trusted with the person's relay session while it lasts.** The bridge never logs, stores or echoes the token (it keeps a sha256 of it, to count its calls), sends it only to the relay and only in the `Authorization` header (never in a URL), and signs nothing but a `pev` act with it. Signing out removes it from the tab and revokes nothing: the relay offers no revocation, so the token stays valid there until it expires. The code arrives in the callback URL's query, where the code flow puts it: it is single use, short-lived, and useless without the verifier, which never leaves the tab.

**Limits.** Every relay call has a 10 s deadline, reads at most 256 KiB, follows no redirect and goes through the SSRF guard (unless the operator's relay is on loopback). A payload to sign is at most 60 KiB, under the verifier's 64 KiB with room for the relay's fields. The relay's `/mcp` answer carries the signed payload twice (as `structuredContent` and again as the text of `content`), so a payload near that size made mostly of characters JSON escapes can make the answer pass the 256 KiB read, and that sign is refused as `relay-unreachable`. Every call this bridge makes comes from its one address, and the relay limits some routes per client address before it checks any credential: `/token` to 60 calls a minute and 50 every 15 minutes, and `/register` to 20 an hour. It sets no per-address limit on `/mcp`, where each made-up token still costs it a token check. So whoever makes this bridge call the relay spends what everyone signing in here shares, a caller who is not signed in included: made-up codes posted to `/ui/oauth/exchange` would spend the token endpoint's budget, after which no one's sign-in through this bridge finishes until it refills. The bridge therefore admits each relay call before it makes it, and asks the relay nothing past these limits. For callers the relay has not accepted here (a code exchange, the sign-in set-up, which is the relay's metadata and a registration, and a sign with an access token the relay has not yet accepted from this bridge): at most 20 calls a minute in all, and 10 from one client address; at most 30 code exchanges every 15 minutes in all, and 10 from one address, so the bridge stays well under the token endpoint's 50; at most 10 registrations an hour (one per process when it works); and at most 8 of these calls at once. An exchange these limits would refuse asks the relay nothing, not even its metadata. A token the relay issued through this bridge counts as a sign-in until it expires, and one the relay accepted in a sign for at most an hour. Its calls count only against that sign-in, 20 a minute, and against its person, 40 a minute over all their sign-ins. The person is the user the relay's signed text names, as the handlers name them (its agent's controller, from the agent's DID document on the identity host, read through the handlers' own ten-minute cache; the agent itself when that document cannot be read), learned at the first sign with the token that succeeds. The relay writes that text for the token's own sign-in, so no one can make a token count against someone else, and one account's tokens share one budget, whichever client each was issued to. No minute budget a signed-in call counts in is shared with anyone else: the relay keeps no per-address limit on `/mcp` for such a budget to stay under, and whoever holds a token can call the relay's `/mcp` directly at any rate, so a cap over every signed-in call would protect nothing and would let anyone with an Interego account (sign-up is open) stop everyone's signing with a few tokens. What people signed in share is the bridge's own work: at most 64 of their calls run at once, and at most 2 of those for one person. A token the relay refused (401 or 403 at `/mcp`) is refused again from memory for a minute, without a relay call. A limit reached is 429 with `retryAfterSeconds` (and `Retry-After`): `relay-calls-limited` for one caller's own limit (an address not signed in, a sign-in, a person), `relay-busy` for the bridge's, as for a 429 from the relay itself.

What remains. Strangers, from enough addresses, can still spend the 20 calls a minute and the 8 places at once for callers not signed in, and the 30 exchanges every 15 minutes, and so delay new sign-ins for as long as they keep it up (each refusal says how long), and, after a restart (which forgets the tokens the bridge knew), each person's first sign. A person already signed in keeps signing, up to their own limits, whatever strangers and other accounts do, with one exception: the 64 calls at once. One person holds at most 2 of them, so filling them takes 32 Interego accounts, each with a token the relay has accepted here and each keeping 2 calls running. Since each person signs at most 40 times a minute, 32 accounts can do that for about 20 of the relay's answers each minute, a few seconds; keeping it full takes enough accounts that their 40 calls a minute, times how long the relay takes to answer one, add up to 64 running: about 300 accounts at a third of a second an answer. A call refused for it is 429 `relay-busy` with `retryAfterSeconds` 1. The counts live in the process. The bridge's 15 minutes start with its first exchange, as the relay's do with the first token request it takes from the bridge's address, so the two windows line up while the bridge runs and nothing else reaches the relay from its outbound address. A restart starts the bridge's counts again inside the relay's window, so strangers can bring the token endpoint up to 60 made-up codes in it, past its 50; the relay then refuses the bridge's code exchanges (429 `relay-busy`, with the relay's wait) until its window ends. Anything else that reaches the relay from the bridge's outbound address shares the relay's per-address budgets with the bridge the same way.

The client address is the one the proxy in front of the bridge saw: `server.ts` trusts one proxy hop whenever it does not listen on loopback (the image listens on every interface behind Railway's edge) and none on loopback, and `PEV_TRUST_PROXY_HOPS` sets another number. A loopback bridge behind a reverse proxy on the same machine (its host guard admits the deployment URL's host, so such a proxy reaches it) sees every caller as that proxy unless `PEV_TRUST_PROXY_HOPS=1`: the per-address limits are then the bridge's own, and one stranger at one address can delay every new sign-in, for 15 minutes with 10 made-up codes, or for a minute at a time with 10 made-up tokens, and each refusal tells the person their own address is limited. A bridge listening on a public interface with no proxy in front, with the default left as it is, lets a caller name its own address in `X-Forwarded-For`, so only the bridge's totals hold; `PEV_TRUST_PROXY_HOPS=0` stops that. The startup says which address the limits count by. The page does not call the relay itself (its policy is `connect-src 'self'`, and the relay's CORS allowlist, as `deploy/mcp-relay/cors-allowlist.ts` is written, does not name this bridge's origin), so a person cannot spend their own address's budget instead. If the relay admitted this origin and the page's policy named the relay, the page could call `/mcp` directly: the access token would no longer pass through the bridge, and these limits would matter less. That is not built. A refusal from these routes is JSON in the bridge's refusal shape; with sign-in off they answer 404 `interego-sign-in-off`.

### Who can take part today

- **A learner needs an Ethereum-style (secp256k1) wallet that does `personal_sign`, and answers one signing prompt per line they say**, or pastes an EIP-191 signature over canonical JSON. There is no passkey (WebAuthn), `did:key`, email, SSO or LMS-launch identity, and no anonymous practice. A typical learner without such a wallet cannot take part yet.
- **Or an Interego account.** With Interego sign-in on (the image turns it on), a person signed in to the Interego relay acts here through this bridge's web UI, Claude, ChatGPT or another relay client, on every route their roles allow, the packet routes included; decisive acts come from this bridge's web UI unless `PEV_CHAT_AGENTS_DECIDE=1`. This is relay-attested, not user-held (see the section above).
- **Everyone is a key or an account.** One person with two keys, or a key and an Interego account, is two learners: attempt numbers, quotas and conflict checks see principals, not people. One Interego account through two agents (Claude and ChatGPT) is one principal.

## Run it

**The offline demo** (no network, no model, no environment variables):

```bash
npx tsx applications/practice-evidence/demo/run.ts                  # walk, print every step, write demos/output/pev-<time>.md
npx tsx applications/practice-evidence/demo/run.ts --serve          # ...then serve the web UI on 127.0.0.1:6110 in demo mode
npx tsx applications/practice-evidence/demo/run.ts --serve --port 6111
```

With `--serve`, open `http://127.0.0.1:6110/ui`. The other person, the judge and the drafter are the scripted fixture: it answers lines it has no script for with "Go on.", judges the demo rubrics from its script and gives other rubrics the lowest level, drafts the same "Renegotiating a deadline with a client" scenario whatever brief you send (the draft's note says no model read it), and says so on every record. There is no `--live` mode; the real path is the server below.

**The bridge:**

```bash
PEV_DEFAULT_POD_URL=https://your-pod.example/practice/ \
PEV_ADMIN_ADDRESSES=0xYourAdminAddress \
PEV_PLATFORM_KEY=0xPlatformKeyHex \
PEV_RECORD_KEY=0xRecordKeyHex \
PEV_LLM_API_KEY=… PEV_LLM_MODEL=… \
  npx tsx applications/practice-evidence/bridge/server.ts
```

Then the web UI is at `/ui`, the affordance manifest at `/affordances`, MCP at `/mcp` and the ontology at `/ns/pev`.

- **The pod** must accept this bridge's writes, or the bridge needs `PEV_POD_WRITE_SECRET` for a gated pod. A refused write is reported as `pod-refused-write`, naming that setting.
- **Ephemeral key.** Without `PEV_PLATFORM_KEY` the bridge makes a key at startup, prints its address, and every record says `keyKind: ephemeral`. After a restart those records are untrusted until the old address is added to `PEV_TRUSTED_PLATFORM_ADDRESSES`. When the pod's registry is signed by a key this bridge does not trust, the startup lines name that key with a warning: trust it only if it is an address this bridge printed before, because anyone who can write the pod can sign a record with a key of their own, and a trusted key signs everything. Otherwise use a fresh pod path.
- **Record key.** With `PEV_RECORD_KEY` set, each record's graph (transcripts, judgments, reviewers' reasons, role entries, who asked for a statement, everything in the record body) is written to the operator pod only as an envelope encrypted to this bridge's own key (`<slug>-graph.envelope.jose.json`, marked `iep:encrypted true`), and the bridge opens it on read and checks the platform signature over the opened graph. A new attempt's IRI is keyed with the same key: it is `urn:pev:attempt:k<fingerprint>:<16 hex>-<n>`, where the 16 hex characters are an HMAC-SHA256, under a secret derived from the record key (label `pev-attempt-iri`), of the pod, the scenario and the learner's address, so only a holder of the key can tell whose an attempt is. The fingerprint is the first 8 hex of HMAC-SHA256 of `pev-iri-key-fingerprint` under that secret: a key identifier, which says which key the IRI was derived under (and, at the end of a sealed record's descriptor URL, which key sealed the record) and reveals nothing about the key or about any learner. Plaintext records written before the key was set stay readable, and a bridge with the key reads both. A record encrypted to a key this bridge does not hold, or read by a bridge with no key, is never read as empty or negative. Every record a bridge seals carries its key's mark (see the next point), so on a pod whose own records carry the mark of a key that is not this bridge's (of any key, when it holds none) every act that reads the pod but `list_scenarios` is refused with 409 `record-key-mismatch` before it reads any record (see the next point). Only where none of the pod's own records carries such a mark (someone who can write the pod rewrote the manifest without them) does the sealed record itself refuse: read on its own it is refused as unreadable (409 `record-not-trusted`, naming `PEV_RECORD_KEY`), never read as an answer such as "not ratified", and `review_queue`, the `get_attempt` list, `scenario_results` and `calibration`, which would count or list without it, answer 409 `records-sealed`, naming `PEV_RECORD_KEY`, with `sealedRecordsSkipped`, when any attempt head on the pod is sealed to a key this bridge does not hold; the three signed ones read the role registry first, so where it is sealed as well they answer 409 `record-not-trusted` about the registry (naming `PEV_RECORD_KEY`) instead, and `scenario_results` reads the scenario's own head before the attempts, so where that head is sealed (and the registry is not) it answers 409 `record-not-trusted` about the scenario, naming `PEV_RECORD_KEY`. `list_scenarios`, with the marks or without them, leaves a sealed scenario out, counts it in `sealedRecordsSkipped` (apart from `untrustedRecordsSkipped`), answers `complete: false` and carries `sealedRecords` naming `PEV_RECORD_KEY`. An envelope that names this bridge's key but does not open with it (anyone can encrypt to a public key: bytes that are not UTF-8, a field that is not base64, a changed ciphertext) is a record that is not trusted (409 `record-not-trusted`, saying why) and is counted with the untrusted records, never reported as an unreachable pod. So is a record whose descriptor or graph the pod serves over this bridge's read limit (8 MiB, by the length it declares or by the bytes it sends): 409 `record-not-trusted` saying it is over the read limit, counted with the untrusted records by `list_scenarios`, `review_queue`, the `get_attempt` list, `scenario_results` and `calibration`, which still answer. Anyone who can write the pod can plant such a file; before, one at an attempt head made `review_queue`, the `get_attempt` list, `scenario_results` and `calibration` answer 502 `pod-unreachable`, and one at a scenario head `list_scenarios`. A record an answer rests on and the bridge cannot read is never read as a negative answer either: when the scenario chain a projection, a review or `get_attempt` reads cannot be read (a version sealed to another key, an envelope that does not open, a graph that is gone, a manifest chaining elsewhere), or the version a standing review targeted cannot be, the act is refused with 409 `record-not-trusted` saying why (a scenario chain with two heads: 409 `chain-forked`), not answered "not ratified" or "not verifiable", and no skip audit is written. A version that is read and fails the trust rule is still skipped, as any untrusted record is.
- **Every bridge on a pod holds the same record key.** A bridge holding another record key, or none, derives other attempt IRIs for the same learners: it would find their keyed attempts absent and start second chains beside them, and what it wrote (sealed to its own key, or in plaintext) would not be under the pod's key. So every record a bridge seals names its key's fingerprint at the end of its descriptor URL (`…-k<fingerprint>.ttl`), as every attempt IRI it keys does, and the pod's manifest says which key its records are under from the first record sealed, a role entry or a scenario draft included, whether or not an attempt was started since. Only the pod's own records count: manifest entries whose descriptor sits directly in the pod's records container (`pev/records/`), where every record a bridge writes for the pod it serves goes and no holder copy does. A holder copy of a keyed attempt (plaintext, under `pev/holder-copies/`), which a learner may have another operator's bridge write into a pod this bridge serves, marks nothing, so this bridge goes on there. When the pod's own records name a key whose fingerprint is not this bridge's (or any key, when this bridge holds none), the bridge refuses every signed act, read or write (every write and start of an attempt, `get_attempt` on one attempt and the list, `review_queue`, `scenario_results`, `export_record`, `holder_copy`, the two packets, `draft_scenario`), and the unsigned `calibration`, with 409 `record-key-mismatch`, naming `PEV_RECORD_KEY`, where the manifest names the other key (`markedAt`, a descriptor URL or an attempt IRI, and `markedBy`), every such key's fingerprint (`podKeys`, the first as `podKey`) and its own (`bridgeKey`, with `bridgeKeyOnPod` when the pod holds records under it too). The check is the first read of the pod, before any record (the role registry included), so the refusal carries these fields whichever of the pod's records is sealed; before, on a pod whose role registry was sealed (as on a deployment, where the key writes every record), `review_queue`, `get_attempt` and `scenario_results` answered 409 `record-not-trusted` about the registry, without them. The reads that still answer on such a bridge: `list_scenarios`, marked `recordKeyMismatch` (one scenario asked for by its IRI with a version sealed is refused with 409 `record-key-mismatch` too), and the two that read no record, `draft_scenario_packet` and, in demo mode only, `sign_as_demo_role`. The startup lines say so. The pod's records say which keys wrote to it, not which key is the operator's: on a pod that holds no sealed record yet, a bridge with the wrong key that writes first marks the pod with its key, and the bridge with the right key is then refused there. So the refusal does not say to switch: it tells a bridge with no key to set the pod's key, and a bridge with a key to check which fingerprint is the operator's (a bridge with a key prints its fingerprint when it starts); on a pod holding records under two keys (two bridges raced, or someone who can write the pod added an entry) the operator decides which key the pod keeps. Plain IRIs and plaintext records carry no mark, so a pod that never had a key works as before, and a bridge given a key writes on a pod of plaintext records written before it. Every act reads what it can be refused on (the attempt, the role registry, the scenario chain) before it writes, so a refused act wrote nothing; where a sealed record carries no mark (a manifest rewritten by someone who can write the pod), the record itself still refuses each act that reads it (409 `record-not-trusted`, naming `PEV_RECORD_KEY`), before any write. An act that reads no sealed record is not refused there: while the role registry is plaintext, such a bridge can register roles, author scenarios and start a newcomer's attempt on a plaintext scenario, in plaintext (a bridge with no key, which marks nothing) or sealed to its own key (a bridge holding another key, whose first sealed record marks the pod with that key, so the bridge holding the pod's key is refused there from then on). The check reads the manifest only, so an entry a pod writer adds to the records container can stop this bridge, its reads as well as its writes, as forking a chain can; it cannot make the bridge write. **Back the key up:** if `PEV_RECORD_KEY` is lost, every encrypted record is unreadable, by this bridge and by anyone else, and nothing can recover it. Without the key, records are plaintext and the startup lines say so. Holder copies are never encrypted: they go to the learner's own pod, and the learner does not hold the bridge's key. On a deployment set `PEV_REQUIRE_RECORD_KEY=1` (the image does), so a key that is missing or blank stops the bridge instead of starting it in plaintext.
- **What stays public with a record key.** Only the graphs are encrypted. Anyone who can read the pod (the repository's own gate serves reads to everyone) still reads every descriptor and the manifest: per record, the platform that signed it, its kind, its modal status, its validity times, its trust level and issuer, the graph IRI it describes, its chain of versions and its content hash (every record body carries 144 bits of fresh randomness, `salt`, so the hash confirms no guess of what a sealed body holds, even for a projection audit, whose other fields are public or one of a few values), and the people it names in a role that is public by design: a scenario's authors and the reviewer who ratified it, an attempt's reviewer (while the review stands), the admin who changed a role entry. No descriptor names a learner; a projection audit names no one. Per attempt, a reader sees its IRI, how many versions it has and when each was written (every exchange is a version, so roughly how long the conversation was), and whether it was reviewed (and by whom), contested, withdrawn or redacted. Attempts of one learner on one scenario share the keyed part of their IRI (`…-1`, `…-2`), so a reader can tell that they are one person's and how many there are, but not whose; every keyed IRI names the same key fingerprint, which tells keyed attempts from plain ones and nothing more, and so does every sealed record's descriptor URL, which tells sealed records from plaintext ones (their graph's file name says so too). Attempts started before the key was set keep their plain IRIs: those stay linkable to their learner's address, and their records stay plaintext. A learner's keyed attempts are numbered from `…-1` whatever she did before the key (the attempt number in the sealed body counts both), so the number of plain attempts a candidate address has, which anyone holding it can count, does not single out her keyed ones.
- **Without a record key** every record is plaintext and every attempt IRI is a plain sha256 of the pod, the scenario and the learner's address: anyone who can read the manifest and holds a candidate address (an Ethereum address is public wherever its owner has used it) finds that learner's attempts, then reads them.
- **Limits.** Calls on this bridge's own model and attempt starts are limited per principal (a key, or a person signed in with their Interego account, whichever agent they act through; never per relay address) and in total per hour (429 `model-budget-spent` or `attempt-budget-spent`, with `retryAfterSeconds`). The counts live in the process: a restart or a second replica starts its own. A per-request key and the packet routes are not counted. A refusal writes nothing to the pod, so a stranger hammering the bridge cannot fill the pod with refusal rows.
- **Reading the pod.** Each response is read up to 8 MiB, and one act reads at most 32 MiB from pods in all (four times that), so planted files cannot make one act hold more: a listing (`list_scenarios`, `review_queue`, the `get_attempt` list, `scenario_results`, `calibration`) that reaches the limit stops reading, answers `complete: false` and carries `readLimitReached`; any other act that reaches it is refused (409 `read-limit-reached`), writing nothing. A record a listing reads and skips (untrusted, misplaced, unreadable) is dropped at once, so an act holds only the records its answer uses. A record for which the pod answers an error status for its descriptor or graph (401, 403, 5xx; for a graph after the substrate's retries) is unreadable, refused on its own as 409 `record-not-trusted` naming the status, and counted with the untrusted records by the listings, which answer `complete: false` because what it holds is unknown; anyone who can write the pod can plant a record whose graph links to a resource this bridge may not read. A descriptor's graph is followed only within the descriptor's own container (where every record has it), never to another host, another pod on the same host, or another path of this pod. Only a request that gets no answer, and a manifest that cannot be read, are 502 (`pod-unreachable`, `pod-refused-read`).
- **What a bridge keeps between acts.** Records are written once, so a record the bridge read, checked and found trusted where it sits is kept for up to 5 minutes (64 MiB in all, least recently used dropped first; a record over 8 MiB is not kept), and an act reads the pod's manifest and only the records it has not seen. Where a kept record sits (its graph, its pod, its place in the chain) is checked against the manifest on every read. So a record someone changes in place on the pod after this bridge verified it is read again when its entry expires (a bridge that has not read it before reads it at once), and the versions under a redacted head are always read again, so a transcript another process removed is never served from what was kept. One act reads the manifest once, and again under its lock only when the same bridge wrote a record meanwhile; a write by another process in between is caught, as before, by the fresh read of the heads just before the publish and by the publish's compare-and-set. On the offline demo flow this took GETs to the pod from 859 to 323 (12.8 to 4.8 per act). The web UI shows a recorded exchange from the act's own answer rather than reading the attempt again, and says when an answer is not the whole pod (`complete: false`) before showing it, without a "none yet" prompt while it is not.
- **Your own key for one request** (`llm_api_key` with `llm_model`) is used once, never stored or echoed. The web UI offers it as a toggle in the header.

## Records and lifecycle

Every record is a Context Descriptor on the operator pod whose graph carries typed `pev:` triples and the whole record body as canonical JSON, signed by the platform key with a content-bound authorship proof. An attempt is one supersede chain on one graph IRI:

```
start_attempt ─► in-conversation  (Hypothetical)
in-conversation ─(say | record_turns)─► in-conversation
in-conversation ─(endAfter | turn limit | end_conversation)─► ended   (transcript hash fixed)
ended ─(judge_attempt | record_judgment)─► judged                       (Hypothetical; platform result)
judged | contested | review-retracted ─(countersign_attempt)─► countersigned   (Asserted)
judged | contested | review-retracted ─(override_attempt)─► overridden         (Asserted)
countersigned | overridden ─(contest_review)─► contested                       (Hypothetical)
countersigned | overridden | contested ─(retract_review)─► review-retracted     (Retracted)
any state but redacted ─(redact_attempt)─► redacted                              (Retracted; earlier graphs deleted)
```

A scenario moves draft → published → ratified (Asserted, attested by the reviewer); a content change is a new, unratified version. Learners start published or ratified versions only.

## Inference and disclosure

`src/model.ts` holds the seam and reads no environment. The Anthropic Messages client sends only `model`, `max_tokens`, `system` and `messages`, with per-request and total timeouts; a 5xx or network failure is retried, nothing else is, and a timeout is reported, not retried. The model the provider returned is the one disclosed. Every failure is a refusal (400, 502 or 503) that records nothing and carries the disclosure of the call that was made.

## Calibration and standing

Calibration is computed on read from the reviewed attempts on the pod and is not published. It is agreement with the reviewer, who usually saw the judge's levels first; the blind subset is declared by the reviewer. Cells are keyed per judge, prompt template hash, rubric version hash and criterion, so a rubric edit (even a weight) starts new cells; a pooled view across versions with the same criterion text is offered for information only. Standing (a reputation-weighted agreement rating of at least 0.9, resting on at least 20 reviewed outcomes confirmed by at least five different people; software agents only) is reported and changes nothing in this version: every attempt still needs a qualified reviewer before it can leave as evidence. `pev.calibration` needs no signature, so it hides every figure that rests on 1 to 4 outcomes, or that would give one by subtraction (a cell's agreement less a subset's, a pooled cell's figure less those of the versions it pools), and keeps the count (see "What a caller who signs nothing gets").

## Results

Per scenario, for reviewers, authors and admins: counts by state, final levels from standing reviews, provisional levels from primary judgments (labelled), coached and attempt-number splits, and what reviewers changed. The page says what results are: what happened in practice, not what caused it.

Suppression, as built: a row of level counts is shown whole or not at all. When any count in it is from 1 to 4, every count in the row is hidden (zeros too) and only the row total stays, so the hidden counts are one of several ways to make that total and none can be worked out by subtraction. A split (coached or not, first attempt or later) is withheld for a criterion when either of its rows is hidden, because the two rows add up to the shown total. The pass-rule count is hidden when it or the rest is from 1 to 4. What reviewers changed is a row of three counts per criterion (raised, lowered, unchanged) under the same rule. Only reviewers and admins, who may read any attempt through `get_attempt`, see each change with its level, reason and time; an author sees the counts. Counts by state (for example "contested: 1") are not suppressed: they say what happened to attempts, not at what level anyone performed. The provisional table is a different measure (the judge's levels over judged attempts) and follows the same rule on its own.

## Projections and what they lose

`xapi` builds an xAPI 2.0 statement; `cmi5` builds the same with cmi5-allowed context only (a derived registration and session id, no cmi5-defined category, so practice cannot satisfy an assignable unit). Both carry the practice marker, the rubric and transcript hashes, the primary judge's model as `provider/model` (as the provider returned it, or as a packet judge declared it, with `declaredBy` naming who), the judge's identity, and the reviewer. A pass gate carries a score and `success`; feedback only carries levels and neither. Each statement lists what it loses (the quotes, the transcript, the reviewer's reasons, other judgments, that a packet judge's model is declared, that the attempt number counts keys, that coaching is declared). SCORM is refused (501): a SCORM write is made by content in the browser, so it would carry a score without its provenance.

## What it does not do yet

- **Screen what goes in.** There is no check on a learner's lines before they reach the model, and no content check when an author publishes: a learner's line goes straight to the model, and any author text becomes the other person's instructions. Do not put this in front of learners who are not your own colleagues until both exist.
- **Identity a person holds beyond wallets.** See "Who can take part today": no passkeys, `did:key`, SSO, LMS launch or anonymous practice. Interego sign-in lets a person without a key act, but relay-attested, not user-held.
- **Keep transcripts private without a record key.** Without `PEV_RECORD_KEY` records are plaintext on the operator pod and attempt IRIs link to a learner's address (see Threat notes). With it the graphs are encrypted and new attempt IRIs are keyed, but descriptors and the manifest stay public (see "What stays public with a record key").
- An overall written feedback text for the learner (feedback is per criterion); all-or-nothing deductions; judge reference material only the judge sees; the judge correcting itself once when a quote does not check out.
- Streaming replies; the other person speaking first beyond the authored first line; behaviour triggers, moods and reply-length settings for the other person.
- Attempt limits set per scenario (only the bridge's hourly limits exist); a results dashboard with rates and averages.
- UI languages other than English, and an accessibility review of the web UI.
- Send statements to a learning record store; LTI grade passback; SCORM; a cmi5 launch from an LMS.
- Packages for authoring tools.
- Voice, avatars, coaches, knowledge retrieval.
- Encrypted holder copies; writing pod access-control documents.
- Retention windows (automatic redaction after a period); a pod-wide feedback-only switch; review links for people without an account.
- Session keys for signing (one wallet prompt per act today).
- Badges or any credential; metering or billing beyond the hourly limits.

## Cost and growth

Every exchange rewrites the whole attempt body, so storage per attempt grows with the square of its length, and every act runs a manifest read, so the work per act grows with the pod. In one run of the offline demo on the build machine an exchange took about 50 to 70 ms on the in-memory pod; steps whose DNS look-up (made by the SSRF guard on every pod request) stalled took about 2 s longer. The demo report lists the per-turn figures of each run. A real pod adds its own round trips.

## Threat notes

- A learner can try to talk the judge into a level. Quote checking cannot catch a real quote used out of context; the reviewer is the control.
- Reviewers usually see the judge's levels first; blind order is declared by the reviewer, not enforced.
- When the learner's own agent wrote the other person's lines, the record says so and the queue raises the attempt.
- **Transcripts are encrypted only with a record key.** With `PEV_RECORD_KEY` set, the pod holds every record's graph only as ciphertext encrypted to that one key, so a reader of the pod, including whoever runs its storage, sees no transcript, judgment or reviewer reason, and cannot tell from an attempt's IRI whose it is. What stays public is listed under "What stays public with a record key": the descriptors and the manifest, which name authors, ratifiers, reviewers and admins but no learner, and per attempt its version count, times, review state and redaction. The key is held only by the bridge: anyone who obtains it reads every encrypted record and can link every keyed attempt to its learner, and anyone who loses it loses every encrypted record. Without the key, records are as public as the pod makes them: they are plaintext and the bridge writes no access-control document, so on a pod that serves reads anonymously (the repository's own write-gated pod does, by design) anyone who can reach the pod can read every transcript, judgment and reviewer reason straight from the manifest, whatever `get_attempt` allows, and find a given learner's attempts from her address alone, because an attempt IRI is then a plain hash of it; the xAPI statement even names the record. Records and attempt IRIs written before the key was set stay as they were. The bridge sends its pod credential on reads as well as writes, so an operator whose pod can require that credential for reads of `pev/` can also keep plaintext records from strangers (proven only against the in-memory pod: no such real pod was tested). Holder copies are never encrypted and go only to pods that accept unauthenticated writes, which in practice are usually world-readable too.
- **Someone who can write the pod but lacks the key** cannot use the bridge to read sealed records. Every act that hands a caller a version's bytes or text, copies it or deletes its graph (`export_record`, `holder_copy`, `redact_attempt`) uses a chain whose every version is trusted and sits where it was written, and `get_attempt` shows no state from a version that does not. A chain is followed only through versions the pod's manifest files under the same graph: a `supersedes` forged to point at another learner's record is refused as unreadable before that record is fetched, and a record filed under two graphs is caught by its signed body as misplaced. Such a writer can still delete or reorder records (truncation, below), which needs an anchor outside the pod.
- The person-versus-agent rules rest entirely on the admin's registry.
- **Spending.** Without limits, any fresh key (or, with Interego sign-in, any Interego account) could make the bridge spend its operator's model key. The bridge's own model is for registered principals by default on the server, and calls and attempt starts are limited per signer and in total per hour in each process. A restart resets the counts, and many fresh keys share only the total. Writes are further bounded by the served pods, the turn limit, five judgments and two contests per attempt.
- **Records count only where they were written.** A record's signed body names its graph, its pod, its place in the chain and the version before it. A platform-signed record copied to another pod, or an old version re-published on top of a newer one, keeps a valid signature but is refused as misplaced (409 `record-not-trusted`). What no reader of the pod alone can catch is truncation: someone who can write the pod can delete the newest versions, and the chain then ends at an older, correctly placed one. That needs an anchor outside the pod, which is not built.
- **Reads are bounded.** Every response is read with an 8 MiB limit (a record's descriptor or graph over it is a record that is not trusted, never an unreachable pod), a descriptor's graph is fetched only from the descriptor's own host, records are read only from under the pod, and every read, signed or not (`list_scenarios` and `calibration` need no signature), answers only for served pods.
- **Text is well-formed or refused.** A signed payload (in either envelope form: a raw `_signed_payload` is checked before it is hashed), a per-request model name or an unsigned brief holding a lone UTF-16 surrogate is refused (400 `payload-shape`, naming the field) before any model call or write: such text cannot be encrypted or hashed as sent. A lone surrogate in what a model returns is recorded as U+FFFD, as a UTF-8 encoder writes it; one that a judge's or drafter's JSON decodes to makes that answer unusable (502), like any other. A record the bridge cannot encrypt is a 500 `record-shape` with nothing written, never reported as the pod's failure.
- The Host-header guard protects a loopback bridge from DNS rebinding: a request naming another site gets 421 on every route except `/health`, which the shared bridge mounts before any middleware and which answers only a status and the build id.
- **Replays.** The nonce ledger is per process, so it does not reach a restart or a second replica. What does: every act on an attempt keeps its payload hash in the record, and an envelope already in the record is refused (409 `envelope-already-used`); a start envelope that already started one of the learner's recent attempts is refused; the role registry carries the payload hashes of the admin envelopes it applied within the replay window. Acts on a review or a scenario are also bound to the exact record or version they name. What remains: an admin envelope that changed nothing is not recorded, so after a restart it could be replayed within its ten minutes if someone captured it in transit.
- **The web UI's sign-in carries a person's relay access token.** Signed in from this bridge's web UI, the relay's OAuth access token for that sign-in passes through the bridge on every act it signs. For its hour it lets the holder call the relay's tools as the person, and the relay trades it for the account token at `/identity-token`, so the operator is trusted with that session while it lasts. Signing out revokes nothing: the relay offers no revocation. The relay also reads every payload it signs in plaintext (see "Signing in from this bridge's web UI"). Every relay call comes from the bridge's one address, so strangers, from enough addresses, can still delay new sign-ins through it while they keep at it (see **Limits.** there). They cannot stop the signing of anyone already signed in, and nor can anyone with an Interego account, since each person's signed-in calls count only in budgets of their own; what people signed in share is 64 calls at once, 2 per person, which 32 accounts signing at the same moment fill for a few seconds a minute. A loopback bridge behind a reverse proxy on the same machine sees every caller as that proxy unless `PEV_TRUST_PROXY_HOPS=1`, and then one stranger can delay every new sign-in.
- **Interego sign-in is relay-attested, not user-held.** A request the relay signs is read as the person whose agent it names when the signature recovers to `PEV_TRUSTED_RELAY_ADDRESSES` and the identity host names a user as that agent's controller. Whoever holds the relay's delegation key, or controls the identity host (or its DNS or TLS), can therefore act as any person here, the admins in `PEV_ADMIN_DIDS` included; every such record says authority `relay-delegated`, with the agent and the relay address. An agent's document is kept for 10 minutes, so an agent the identity host stops naming can act for up to that long. The chat-agent limit on decisive acts rests on the OAuth client's self-chosen name. Turning the route off (`PEV_TRUSTED_RELAY_ADDRESSES` empty) stops sign-in at once, and the reviews it attested no longer verify for projection.
- Redaction deletes graphs but leaves descriptors and manifest entries, which hold proofs and hashes, not text. The kept hashes are plain sha256 of each line and quote, so anyone who can read the redacted record can confirm a guess of a short or predictable line ("Yes.", "I understand.") against them; the redaction note says so. Without a record key that is anyone who can read the pod; with one, only whoever holds the key. Keyed commitments are not built.
- **Demo mode** writes to the operator pod it is given and signs with an ephemeral platform key it refuses to run beside a configured one; a page that reaches the loopback UI can act as any demo role. Use a demo pod path.

## Deviations

From the design decisions and the spec, deliberately:

1. **No grants in this version.** Standing is computed and shown; it changes nothing, and there is no grant record to revoke.
2. **Records are encrypted only with `PEV_RECORD_KEY`**, and then only their graphs, to the bridge's own key; descriptors and the manifest stay public. Without the key they are plaintext, the pod's own access control governs reads, and on a pod that serves reads anonymously that means anyone (see Threat notes). Encrypted holder copies are out of scope.
3. **The nonce ledger is per process**; replays across processes are caught by the payload hashes the records keep (see Threat notes).
4. **Concurrency.** Every record has a unique slug, so a race between processes becomes a detectable fork (409 `chain-forked`, no repair path in this version), never a silent overwrite. The heads are read again right before every publish. Graph locks belong to each handler set, so two handler sets in one process behave like two processes.
5. **SCORM is 501.** `cmi5` is narrowed to xAPI with cmi5-allowed context.
6. **Nothing is posted to an LRS.**
7. **The rubric is public**, including to the learner.
8. **Ephemeral platform keys** make earlier records untrusted after a restart until trusted again.
9. **No server-side model fallback**, and no `thinking` or sampling parameters.
10. **Calibration is computed on read**, not published.
11. **Envelope form B** is accepted only when `agent_id` names the recovered signer, or when the signature recovers to a trusted relay address (Interego sign-in, relay-attested: the person is the agent's controller on the identity host).
12. **Holder copies** go only to pods that accept the bridge's unauthenticated writes, never to the operator pod or a pod this bridge serves (400 `target-pod-is-served`: copies there would add competing heads to the attempt's own chain). `pev.export_record` gives the learner the signed bytes in every case.
13. **Several judgments per attempt** (up to five); the first is primary.
14. **Explicit per-criterion review**: countersign and override both list every criterion.
15. **The verb is `completed`**, with `success` for a pass gate.
16. **Calibration cells are split by prompt template hash** as well as rubric version and criterion.
17. **Blind review is declared, not enforced.**
18. **Redaction** deletes earlier graphs only under the operator pod's path; anything else is listed in `graphsStillPresent`.
19. **Wallet signing is one prompt per act.**
20. **Served pods** bound where the platform key's records are read and written.
21. **Unratified rubrics** cannot be projected.
22. **Additions to the spec's interface:** refusal codes `chat-agent-may-not-decide` (with `agentId`), the verifier's `relay-not-trusted`, `agent-not-on-identity-host`, `agent-document-unreadable` (502), `agent-document-mismatch`, `agent-has-no-user` and `payload-too-large`, `payload-shape`, `pod-url-differs`, `target-missing`, `why-too-long`, `brief-too-long`, `source-text-too-long`, `byok-incomplete`, `group-by-unknown`, `target-pod-is-served`, `records-sealed`, `record-key-mismatch`, `read-limit-reached` (with `readLimitBytes`); output fields `readLimitReached` (the act's read limit and a note) on `list_scenarios`, `review_queue`, the `get_attempt` list, `scenario_results` and `calibration` when that limit left records unread, `sealedRecordsSkipped` and `sealedRecords` on `list_scenarios`, `recordKeyMismatch` on `list_scenarios`, `markedAt`, `markedBy`, `podKey`, `podKeys`, `bridgeKey` and `bridgeKeyOnPod` on `record-key-mismatch`, `currentTarget` on every attempt write, `primaryJudgeLevels` on reviews, the rubric on queue entries, `validFrom`/`validUntil` on a withdrawal, `provisionalLabel` on judgments, `scenarioKey` in scenario views (the web UI needs it to edit a scenario), `k`, `smallCountRule` and per-cell `ratesSuppressed` on calibration, `trusted: false` on a `get_attempt` version that is not trusted where it sits, `interegoSignIn` in `bridge`, `relayAddresses` and `identityHost` on `export_record`, `authority`, `principal`, `agentId` and `relaySigner` on a stored envelope (`authority`, `agentId`, `relaySigner` on an attempt act) made through Interego sign-in, and `signedAs` (the principal, the authority, and for a relay-delegated read the agent and the relay address) on every `get_attempt` answer. The web UI's sign-in routes (`/ui/oauth/*`, `/ui/relay/sign`) are not affordances; their refusal codes are `interego-sign-in-off`, `interego-bearer-missing`, `interego-sign-in-expired`, `oauth-code-shape`, `pkce-verifier-shape`, `oauth-state-shape`, `oauth-code-refused`, `relay-unreachable`, `relay-busy`, `relay-calls-limited`, `relay-metadata-unusable`, `relay-registration-unavailable`, `relay-registration-refused`, `relay-token-unusable`, `relay-refused-signing`, `relay-signed-something-else`, `interego-sign-in-failed` (500), `payload-shape`, `payload-too-large` and `body-unreadable`. Each attempt act keeps its signature, so every act can be re-verified from the record. An untrusted attempt head answers `project_attempt` with 409 `record-not-trusted` and writes no skip audit, and so does a scenario chain or review target the bridge cannot read. Eligibility checks the author-test rule first.
23. **The other person's reply has no length limit**; the 4000-character limit applies to the learner's lines and to lines recorded from a packet.
24. **Demo role keys** on the real server are registered against `PEV_DEFAULT_POD_URL` at startup.
25. **The offline demo has no live mode**: `runDemo({ live: true })` and `--live` say what to run instead. Its step table follows the spec's, with extra checks: 27b reads the skip audit back from the pod; 31a shows that the same reviewer is refused as an earlier reviewer (403) before any state check, and 31b that a different reviewer is refused because the attempt is already reviewed (409); 47b, 50b and the last step read the attempts.
26. **The scripted judge** gives criteria it has no script for the lowest level, with a rationale saying it is a fixture, so a scenario written in `--serve` mode can be judged offline.
27. **The quote rule is stricter than DECISIONS #4**: a quote must also start and end on whole words and hold at least two words (or one word of 12 letters or digits), because a mid-word match can invert what the learner said. The lowest level with no quote is `no-quote-needed`, a third verification status; it counts as the judge's level and does not block countersigning.
28. **The judge does not see the stakes.** Its request carries criteria, levels and descriptors only: no weights, level values, pass rule or use.
29. **Withdrawing a review retracts the attempt's head record.** The head after a withdrawal is modal Retracted (with `validUntil`), although only the review was withdrawn and the transcript and judgments still stand. This is a deliberate encoding: the claim the head made (a reviewed result) no longer stands. A generic reader that trusts modal status alone will treat the whole attempt as withdrawn.
30. **Reads, signed or not, are limited to served pods**; any public pod is an explicit `PEV_SERVED_PODS=*`, and with no pod configured nothing is served. Whether a pod is served is decided from its URL alone, before its name is resolved; only a served pod (or a holder pod a learner copies to) is then checked to resolve to public addresses.
31. **A reviewer who asked for the primary judgment, or had any judgment made by a model they chose** (their own key), may not review that attempt, as a judge signer may not. A reviewer who only asked for a second opinion with the bridge's model still may.
32. **Queue weights**: `unverified` is 0.2 (the spec's 0.25) to make room for `notQuoted` at 0.05; they still add up to 1.
33. **Results suppression** hides whole rows and withholds splits (see Results), and reasons for changes go to reviewers and admins only; the spec suppressed cells.
34. **Hourly limits and the registered-only model route** are additions; a limit refusal writes no audit row (it would let a stranger fill the pod).
35. **Records carry their place in the chain** (`seq`, `prev`) in the signed body, and the role registry carries recent admin envelope hashes.
